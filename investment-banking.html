How an investment bank earns its money

An investment bank sells two things: advice on deals and access to capital. Most of what it earns is paid only when a deal closes. Here are the four places the money comes from, the process that produces it and the valuation that decides the price.

Four fee pools

Every fee a bank's investment banking division earns falls into one of four products. Two are advice and selling securities; two are lending money or arranging it.

  • M&A advisory

    Advising a company on buying, selling or merging. The bank runs the process and negotiates the price.

    Paid mainly as a success fee when the deal completes.

  • Equity capital markets

    Selling new shares for a company: IPOs, follow-on offerings, convertibles.

    Paid as a spread: a percentage of the money raised.

  • Debt capital markets

    Selling bonds for companies, banks and governments to investors.

    Paid as an underwriting fee, smaller per deal, many more deals.

  • Syndicated loans

    Arranging a large loan and sharing it out among a group of lenders.

    Paid as arrangement fees, often tied to financing an acquisition.

Global investment banking fees, Jan–Sep 2024

US$ billions · LSEG Deals Intelligence

Bonds are the biggest pool. M&A comes second.

In the first nine months of 2024 the industry earned $85.3bn. Debt underwriting brought in the most, $31.3bn, because companies refinance all the time. M&A advisory came second with $23.0bn, and it is the pool most sensitive to confidence: it needs boards willing to buy.

Geography matters as much as product: the Americas alone earned more than half. This is the most recent full split by product and region I could verify from LSEG's own report.

2026: equity is back

Fees reached $117bn in the first nine months of 2026, up 12%. Equity underwriting grew fastest, +37%, as listings and follow-ons returned. M&A advisory rose 15%.

The year was not smooth. Third-quarter fees fell 19% from the second quarter as activity pulled back.

A sell-side deal, step by step

Most M&A fees come from processes like this one: a company's owners hire a bank to sell it. Each step narrows the buyers and raises the commitment.

  1. Step 1

    Pitch

    Banks compete for the mandate with a view on value, likely buyers and timing.

  2. Step 2

    Mandate

    The engagement letter fixes the fee: often a monthly retainer plus a success fee on the deal value.

  3. Step 3

    Preparation

    The team builds the financial model, the teaser, the information memorandum and the buyer list.

  4. Step 4

    First round

    Buyers sign an NDA, read the memorandum and send non-binding indicative offers.

  5. Step 5

    Second round

    A shortlist gets the data room, management presentations and time for due diligence.

  6. Step 6

    Binding bids

    Final offers come with a marked-up purchase agreement. The bank negotiates price and terms.

  7. Step 7

    Signing

    Buyer and seller sign the sale and purchase agreement and announce the deal.

  8. Step 8

    Closing

    Regulators and shareholders approve, money changes hands and the success fee is paid.

Five methods, one price

Trading comparables price the company like similar listed companies. Precedent transactions use what buyers paid for similar companies, so they include a control premium and sit higher.

A DCF values the cash the company will generate. An LBO asks what a private-equity fund could pay and still earn its target return, which works as a floor. The 52-week range shows where the market has priced it.

A seller wants an offer near the top of precedent transactions. A board that recommends an offer below most ranges has to explain why.

Who earns it

A few global banks take a large share. In 2024 JP Morgan led with 8.0% of all fees. By the first nine months of 2026 it had earned $10.1bn, with Goldman Sachs second on $8.2bn.

The top ten held 41.8% of the pool in 2026, 3.4 points more than a year earlier. Independent advisers such as Evercore compete mainly in M&A advice, because they don't lend.

Where an analyst fits

The deal team is a ladder. Titles vary by bank; the split of work is similar everywhere.

  1. Years 1–3

    Analyst

    Models, comparables, pitch books and the data room. Where I'm aiming.

  2. Years 3–6

    Associate

    Checks the analysts' work and runs the day-to-day of the process.

  3. Years 6–9

    Vice President

    Manages the deal and the client's team, and keeps the timetable.

  4. Years 9–12

    Director

    Leads execution and starts to bring in mandates.

  5. 12+ years

    Managing Director

    Owns client relationships and wins the business.

Typical ranges, not rules. They differ by bank and country.

Blind spots

League tables are the industry's scoreboard, and they have blind spots.

Many fees are estimates

Banks rarely disclose the fee on each deal, so LSEG estimates many of them from deal size and type. Treat a bank's total as a careful estimate.

Only investment banking

These pools leave out sales and trading, research and wealth management, businesses that the same banks also run.

The 2024 split has moved

Equity fees grew 37% in 2026, so ECM is a bigger slice today than in the product chart above.

Announced is not completed

The $4.6tn of 2025 M&A is announced deals. Some will be blocked or abandoned, and the fee only arrives at completion.
