4 Numbers

How to read them

What each number means, a rule of thumb for each, and how it can mislead you. Illustrated with simple round numbers.

Educational content; not financial advice. The examples use invented figures.

The four numbers

1. Margins

A high, steady margin means the company sets its prices.

Net margin is what is left of each sale after every cost, interest and tax. Compare it with the same company five years ago and with its closest rivals, not with the whole market.

  • Above 15% is strong. Below 5% leaves little room for error.
  • Look for stability. A margin that jumps once may be a one-off gain.
  • Gross margin, before running costs, tells you about pricing power alone.

2. Return on equity

How hard the owners’ money works.

ROE is net profit divided by average equity. It is the cleanest test of whether growth creates value or just consumes capital.

  • Above 15% is strong, below 8% is weak.
  • Debt lifts ROE without improving the business. Read it next to the margin.
  • Buybacks shrink equity and can push ROE very high. Check why.

3. Free cash flow

Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact.

Free cash flow is operating cash minus capital spending. It is what the company could pay out or save without hurting the business.

  • Judge it as a share of sales. Above 10% is strong.
  • A weak year can be a big investment, not a weak business. Check the trend.
  • Compare it with net profit. Profit far above cash is a warning.

4. Price against value

A great company can still be a poor purchase.

I estimate what a share is worth from ten years of free cash flow, discounted back to today. Then I compare it with the price.

  • Negative means the price sits below my estimate. A 20% gap or more is my comfort zone.
  • The estimate moves a lot with its assumptions. Try your own in the Calculator.
  • It reads the past. It cannot see a new competitor or a change in strategy.

Glossary

Net margin
Net profit divided by sales.
Gross margin
Sales minus the direct cost of what was sold, divided by sales.
Equity
What the owners have in the company: assets minus liabilities.
ROE
Return on equity: net profit divided by average equity.
Operating cash flow
Cash the business generates from its normal activity.
Capex
Capital spending on plants, machines, software and the like.
Free cash flow
Operating cash flow minus capex.
FCF yield
Free cash flow divided by market capitalisation.
DCF
Discounted cash flow: future cash flows brought back to today’s value.
Margin of safety
The gap between price and estimated value, kept as protection against being wrong.

Questions

Why do so many great companies show as expensive?

The default estimate is deliberately cautious: free cash flow grows 5% a year for ten years, discounted at 9%. A business that compounds faster, or that you would accept a lower return on, is worth more than that. So read the price-against-value number as a stress test, not a price target. The Scorecard also shows a value range from 3% to 8% growth, and the Lab’s Expectations chart shows the exact growth a price needs, with both assumptions adjustable.

Why does Walmart pass with a 3% margin when Visa needs 20%?

Because a good margin depends on the business. A supermarket earns little on each sale and sells a huge volume. A payments network earns a large cut of every transaction. So retail, energy, payments and technology have their own green and red lines. The other sectors use the general ones: 5% and 15% for margin. The scorecard says when a sector setting is in use.

How do you compare companies that report in different currencies?

For companies outside the United States I use their shares listed in New York, which trade in dollars. Their statements, in euros, kroner, yuan or yen, are converted to dollars at the latest exchange rate. Margins and returns do not change with currency. The value per share does, so a big currency swing can move the estimate without anything changing in the business.

Why are there no banks or insurers?

Their cash flow includes deposits and loans, so free cash flow means something different and the method would mislead. They need their own tools, such as return on tangible equity and capital ratios. I leave them out instead of forcing them into four numbers.

Can I trust the verdict for a cyclical company like Exxon?

Treat it with care. Oil prices swing profit and cash flow from year to year, so I use the average of the last three years of free cash flow for the value estimate. The trend notes under the verdict tell you whether the latest year was unusual.

Is this investment advice?

No. It is an educational screen built from public financial statements. It does not know your goals, your time frame or your risk. Do your own research.

Where does the data come from?

From a public market data provider, reloaded automatically: prices every weekday, financials on Saturdays. Each page shows the update date and the source.

Why does a famous company show as expensive?

The value estimate uses cautious assumptions: 5% growth for ten years and a 9% discount rate. Strong brands often trade above that. Change the assumptions in the Calculator to see how sensitive the answer is.

Why is a number missing?

Some companies do not report a line the calculation needs, or the provider has no data for them. I leave it empty instead of guessing.
